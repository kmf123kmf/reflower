Chapter 1
The rain had been falling since before dawn, a thin grey curtain that hung over the harbour and blurred the masts of the fishing boats into a single smudge of charcoal. Margaret stood at the window of the old customs house with her hands pressed flat against the cold glass, watching for a sail that she already knew would not come.
She had been told, of course. Everyone in the village had made a point of telling her, in the careful, well-meaning voices people reserve for the bereaved and the slightly mad. The Albatross was three weeks overdue; the storm off the Hebrides had been the worst in forty years; there was no sense in hoping.
"You'll catch your death standing there," said Mrs. Pennywhistle from the doorway.
"Then I shall have caught something, at least."
The older woman clicked her tongue and came to stand beside her. For a long moment neither of them spoke. Somewhere below, a door banged in the wind, and a dog began to bark with the monotonous persistence of an animal that has forgotten why it started.
"He wrote to me," Margaret said at last. "Before they sailed. Did I ever tell you that? A ridiculous letter, all about the weather and the cook's terrible porridge and a gull that had taken a liking to his hat."
"Sounds like him."
"It does." She smiled, though it did not reach her eyes. "It sounds exactly like him."
By noon the rain had thinned to a drizzle and the light had taken on that peculiar pewter quality that belongs only to coastal towns in late October. Margaret walked the length of the quay twice, past the lobster pots stacked like the vertebrae of some enormous beast, past the chandler's shop with its faded sign, past the bench where old Tom Hallett sat every afternoon regardless of the weather, mending nets that no one would ever use again.
"Afternoon, Miss Carrow."
"Afternoon, Tom."
"Bit of a blow last night."
"Yes."
He looked up at her then, his eyes pale and watery beneath the brim of his cap. "They'll be sheltering, like as not. Up past Stornoway--there's coves up there a man could hide a fleet in. My own father was lost nine days once, and came home with a beard down to here and a barrel of salt herring he'd traded off a Norwegian."
It was a kindness, and she recognised it as one. She thanked him and walked on, and did not let herself cry until she had turned the corner by the church and there was no one to see.
That night she dreamed of the sea. Not the grey, rain-pocked sea of the harbour, but a vast and luminous expanse under a sky crowded with stars, and on it a single ship with every sail set, moving silently northward toward a horizon that kept retreating, and retreating, and never came any nearer at all.
In the morning there was a knock at the door.
