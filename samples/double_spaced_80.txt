Chapter 1



The rain had been falling since before dawn, a thin grey curtain that hung over

the harbour and blurred the masts of the fishing boats into a single smudge of

charcoal. Margaret stood at the window of the old customs house with her hands

pressed flat against the cold glass, watching for a sail that she already knew

would not come.


She had been told, of course. Everyone in the village had made a point of

telling her, in the careful, well-meaning voices people reserve for the bereaved

and the slightly mad. The Albatross was three weeks overdue; the storm off the

Hebrides had been the worst in forty years; there was no sense in hoping.


"You'll catch your death standing there," said Mrs. Pennywhistle from the

doorway.


"Then I shall have caught something, at least."


The older woman clicked her tongue and came to stand beside her. For a long

moment neither of them spoke. Somewhere below, a door banged in the wind, and a

dog began to bark with the monotonous persistence of an animal that has

forgotten why it started.


"He wrote to me," Margaret said at last. "Before they sailed. Did I ever tell

you that? A ridiculous letter, all about the weather and the cook's terrible

porridge and a gull that had taken a liking to his hat."


"Sounds like him."


"It does." She smiled, though it did not reach her eyes. "It sounds exactly like

him."


By noon the rain had thinned to a drizzle and the light had taken on that

peculiar pewter quality that belongs only to coastal towns in late October.

Margaret walked the length of the quay twice, past the lobster pots stacked like

the vertebrae of some enormous beast, past the chandler's shop with its faded

sign, past the bench where old Tom Hallett sat every afternoon regardless of the

weather, mending nets that no one would ever use again.


"Afternoon, Miss Carrow."
